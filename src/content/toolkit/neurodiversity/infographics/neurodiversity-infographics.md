### Autism

#### In short

Autistic people may find social cues hard, need routine, and feel noise or light strongly. They do feel empathy, but may show it in different ways. Be direct, lower lights and noise, and give extra time to answer.

#### Symptoms

- Difficulty with eye contact, social cues or small talk.
- Strong need for routine or repetitive movements (stimming).
- Over or under-sensitive to noise, light or textures.

#### Age & Gender

- Signs often appear by age 2 or 3, but diagnosis can happen at any age.
- Diagnosed more often in males.
- Females are frequently missed because they are better at masking symptoms.

#### Common myths

- People with autism feel empathy, but may show it differently.
- It’s a lifelong condition and not just for children.
- Not everyone is a genius. Abilities vary widely.

#### Diagnosis

- Diagnosis is based on observing behaviour and developmental history.
- Usually involves paediatricians, psychologists and speech therapists.

#### Professional support

- Speech and Occupational Therapy help with daily living skills.
- There's no medication for autism itself.
- Peer support groups are vital for mental health.

#### How to help

- Be direct and literal. Avoid sarcasm or hints.
- Lower lights and noise to reduce sensory overwhelm.
- Allow extra time for them to process information and respond.

### ADHD

#### In short

ADHD affects focus, memory, time, and sitting still. It isn’t bad behaviour, and many adults have it. Clear written instructions and movement breaks help.

#### Symptoms

- Easily distracted, forgetful, or difficulty managing time, focus, and tasks.
- Fidgeting, inability to sit still, or a sense of inner restlessness.
- Interrupting others, difficulty waiting one's turn, or acting without thinking.

#### Age & Gender

- Symptoms usually appear before age 12, but persist into adulthood.
- Diagnosed more often in males.
- Females are often missed as they tend to have inattentive type ADHD, rather than hyperactive-impulsive type.

#### Common myths

- It’s not 'bad behaviour' or lack of willpower.
- It’s not just a childhood phase. Many adults live with undiagnosed ADHD.
- It’s not caused by bad parenting, too much sugar, or screen time.

#### Diagnosis

- Requires a referral from a GP to a specialist (usually a psychiatrist).
- The assessment involves interviews, questionnaires, and reviewing childhood history to see if symptoms impact daily life.

#### Professional support

- Medication can help improve focus and reduce impulsivity.
- Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) or ADHD coaching helps with organisation and emotional regulation.

#### How to help

- Give clear, written instructions rather than long verbal lists.
- Allow headphones or fidget tools to support those with sensory needs.
- Permit movement breaks, and try to avoid punishing timekeeping errors.

### OCD

#### In short

With OCD, unwanted thoughts cause anxiety, and repeated actions or thoughts only ease it for a short time. It’s a serious condition, and people can’t simply choose to stop. Don’t rush or criticise them, and don’t join in with rituals or offer reassurance.

#### Symptoms

- Unwanted, intrusive thoughts causing anxiety.
- Anxiety is relieved through repetitive physical or purely mental actions.
- The relief is temporary, then the anxiety cycle returns.

#### Age & Gender

- Can start in childhood, but normally begins in late teens or early adulthood.
- Affects males and females roughly equally.
- The average diagnosis occurs 9 to 13 years after initial symptoms start.

#### Common myths

- It’s a serious condition, not just liking things neat.
- People can’t simply choose to stop.
- People with OCD often know their fears are irrational but still feel the anxiety.

#### Diagnosis

- GP refers to a specialist mental health team for a detailed assessment.
- Doctors use medical criteria to identify obsessions and compulsions.
- Diagnosed when symptoms significantly impact daily life.

#### Professional support

- CBT with exposure therapy helps face fears without rituals.
- Antidepressants called selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors can reduce symptom intensity.
- Peer support groups help reduce isolation.

#### How to help

- Do not criticise or rush them during a ritual.
- Do not join in with their compulsions or offer reassurance.
- Keep a calm atmosphere to help lower general anxiety levels.

### Dyslexia

#### In short

Dyslexia makes reading, spelling, and working with the sounds in words harder. It has nothing to do with intelligence. Clear fonts, soft background colours, and extra time help.

#### Symptoms

- Difficulty learning to read, spelling, and decoding text.
- Trouble hearing or manipulating sounds in words.
- Challenges with sequencing, directions, and memory.

#### Age & Gender

- Often identified in primary school when children begin to learn to read and write.
- Historically diagnosed more in males, but current research suggests it affects all genders equally.

#### Common myths

- Dyslexia is a language processing difference, not a visual problem (seeing letters backwards).
- It has absolutely no connection to low intelligence or laziness.

#### Diagnosis

- Carried out by a specialist assessor or an Educational Psychologist.
- Assessments test memory, speed, and the ability to process word sounds.

#### Professional support

- Assistive technology like text-to-speech software, screen readers, or coloured overlays.
- Structured, multisensory teaching methods help improve literacy skills.

#### How to help

- Use plain, sans-serif fonts and pastel backgrounds, not bright white.
- Give verbal cues or diagrams rather than long text.
- Allow extra time for reading and processing.

### Dyspraxia

#### In short

Dyspraxia makes balance, coordination, and planning movements hard. Children don’t grow out of it, and it can affect memory and staying organised too. Break physical tasks into small steps, and don’t criticise people for dropping things.

#### Symptoms

- Poor balance, coordination and general clumsiness.
- Difficulty with fine motor skills like handwriting or tying shoelaces.
- Trouble planning and organising physical movements.

#### Age & Gender

- Symptoms usually appear in early childhood.
- Diagnosed more often in males.
- Females are often missed as avoiding active play or having better handwriting can mask issues.

#### Common myths

- It’s not just being 'clumsy' or lazy.
- Children will not simply grow out of it.
- It doesn’t only affect movement, it often impacts organisation and memory too.

#### Diagnosis

- Assessment by a paediatrician, occupational therapist, or physiotherapist.
- Tests measure motor skills compared to age norms.

#### Professional support

- Occupational Therapy helps improve daily living skills.
- Physiotherapy helps improve core strength and balance.
- Adaptations like using a laptop can replace handwriting.

#### How to help

- Break complex physical tasks into smaller steps.
- Allow extra time for movements like dressing or eating.
- Avoid criticism for messiness or dropping things.

### Dyscalculia

#### In short

Dyscalculia makes numbers, sums, telling the time, and counting money harder. It isn’t just being ‘bad at maths’, and it’s not a sign of low intelligence. Number charts, extra time, and no sudden mental maths in front of others help.

#### Symptoms

- Difficulty understanding number concepts and arithmetic.
- Trouble telling time, counting change or estimating quantities.
- High anxiety when faced with mathematical tasks.

#### Age & Gender

- Signs appear early, often when learning to count or recognise numbers.
- Research suggests it affects males and females equally.

#### Common myths

- It’s not just being 'bad at maths' or lacking effort.
- It’s a neurological difference, not a sign of low intelligence.
- It’s not rare, just less frequently diagnosed than dyslexia.

#### Diagnosis

- Assessment by a specialist teacher or Educational Psychologist.
- Standardised tests measure number sense and calculation ability against age norms.

#### Professional support

- Multisensory teaching using physical objects like counters.
- Technological aids like calculators or voice-activated apps.
- Focus on practical maths needed for daily life (for example, money, time).

#### How to help

- Provide reference charts or visual aids for numbers.
- Allow extra time for any task involving data or figures.
- Avoid asking them sudden mental maths questions in front of others.

### Tourette’s Syndrome

#### In short

Tics are movements or sounds that people with Tourette’s syndrome can’t control. Most people with it don’t swear, and they can’t simply stop their tics. Ignore the tics, and don’t stare, laugh, or tell them to stop.

#### Symptoms

- Involuntary movements like blinking, shrugging or head jerking (motor tics).
- Involuntary sounds like grunting, throat clearing or words (vocal tics).
- An uncomfortable physical urge or sensation before the tic happens.

#### Age & Gender

- Symptoms usually appear between ages 5 and 9.
- Males account for 75% to 80% of diagnoses.

#### Common myths

- Most people do not swear (coprolalia affects only about 10% of cases).
- Tics are not done for attention or bad behaviour.
- People cannot simply stop their tics.

#### Diagnosis

- Diagnosed when a person has multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic.
- Symptoms must have lasted for more than a year.

#### Professional support

- Comprehensive Behavioural Intervention for Tics teaches strategies to manage tic urges.
- Medication may be used if tics cause pain, injury, or severe distress.
- Support groups help reduce stigma and improve self-esteem.

#### How to help

- Ignore the tics completely. Do not stare, laugh, or comment.
- Do not tell them to stop or ask them to be quiet.
- Help reduce stress, as strong emotions can increase tics.

### Anxiety

#### In short

Anxiety can cause dread, racing thoughts, and a pounding heart, and it’s a medical condition, not drama. People can’t just choose to stop feeling anxious. Listen without judging, and ask what helps before a panic attack happens.

#### Symptoms

- Constant feeling of dread, racing thoughts or feeling tense and unable to relax.
- Heart palpitations, sweating, shaking or shortness of breath.
- Avoiding situations or places to escape the worry.

#### Age & Gender

- Can develop at any age, though frequently starts in adolescence.
- Females are roughly twice as likely to be diagnosed as males.

#### Common myths

- It's a medical condition, not drama or attention-seeking.
- People can't just choose to stop feeling anxious or stop worrying.
- Panic attacks feel life-threatening. They aren’t an overreaction.

#### Diagnosis

- GP or psychiatrist assessment using questionnaires.
- Generalised anxiety disorder is diagnosed when uncontrollable worry persists for 6 months and impacts daily life.

#### Professional support

- CBT helps change negative thought patterns.
- Medication can manage physical symptoms.
- Mindfulness and exercise support self-care.

#### How to help

- Listen without judging or saying 'just calm down'.
- Ask what helps them during a panic attack before one happens.
- Understand that plans may change last minute if they feel overwhelmed.

### Dysgraphia

#### In short

With dysgraphia, handwriting is hard to read and holding a pen can hurt. It isn’t just messy writing or laziness, and it doesn’t mean low intelligence. Let them type or use speech-to-text, and give printed notes so they don’t have to copy.

#### Symptoms

- Handwriting is often illegible and poorly spaced.
- Holding a pen causes physical pain or cramping.
- Written work is noticeably weaker than their strong verbal skills.

#### Age & Gender

- It is usually identified in early childhood when learning to write.
- It affects all genders but is historically diagnosed more in males.

#### Common myths

- It is a neurological disconnect, not just 'messy handwriting' or laziness.
- It does not mean a person has low intelligence.
- They can have excellent physical coordination for everything else.

#### Diagnosis

- Diagnosed by an Educational Psychologist or Occupational Therapist.
- Assessments test fine motor skills and compare verbal ability to written output.

#### Professional support

- There is no medication for dysgraphia.
- Occupational therapy improves hand strength and pencil grip.
- Peer support helps individuals share practical technology workarounds.

#### How to help

- Allow the use of typing or speech-to-text software.
- Provide extra time for written tasks or accept verbal submissions.
- Provide pre-printed notes so they do not have to copy notes down.

### Sensory Processing Disorder

#### In short

Some people’s brains find sounds, lights, touch, and other senses hard to handle. This can feel overwhelming, and it isn’t bad behaviour. Quiet spaces, fewer triggers, and things like headphones can help.

#### Symptoms

- Over or under-sensitive to sensory stimuli.
- Difficulty filtering background noise.
- Emotional dysregulation when overwhelmed.

#### Age & Gender

- Often identified in early childhood.
- Diagnosed more often in males.
- Females are often missed due to masking.

#### Common myths

- It’s not bad behaviour, sensory input is genuinely overwhelming.
- Not the same as autism, though they often co-occur.
- Not something people can overcome just by trying harder.

#### Diagnosis

- It isn't a separate diagnosis in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, the main book doctors use to diagnose conditions.
- Often identified by an occupational therapist.
- Frequently co-occurs with other conditions.

#### Professional support

- Sensory integration therapy with an Occupational Therapist.
- A personalised 'sensory diet' of daily activities.
- Environmental adjustments to reduce stimuli.

#### How to help

- Offer quiet or low-stimulation workspaces.
- Reduce sensory triggers like flickering lights.
- Allow headphones, sunglasses, or fidget tools.

### Auditory Processing Disorder

#### In short

With auditory processing disorder, the brain finds it hard to make sense of speech, mostly in noisy places. The ears work fine, and it’s not a sign of low intelligence. Face the person, cut background noise, and write down spoken instructions too.

#### Symptoms

- Difficulty understanding speech in noisy environments.
- Mishearing words, even with normal hearing ability.
- Needing instructions repeated or written down.

#### Age & Gender

- Often identified in childhood, but can persist into adulthood.
- Diagnosed more often in males.
- Females are often missed due to masking.

#### Common myths

- It’s not a problem with the ears.
- It’s not the same as ADHD, though they often co-occur.
- It’s not a sign of low intelligence or inattention.

#### Diagnosis

- Diagnosed by an audiologist, not a GP.
- Tests measure how the brain processes sound.
- Often identified alongside dyslexia or ADHD.

#### Professional support

- Auditory training exercises to improve processing.
- Environmental changes to reduce background noise.
- Assistive technology such as live captions or remote microphones.

#### How to help

- Face the person when speaking, and speak clearly and slowly.
- Reduce background noise in meetings where possible.
- Provide written follow-ups to verbal instructions.

This information isn't a diagnostic tool. If you recognise these symptoms in yourself or others, please contact a doctor.
